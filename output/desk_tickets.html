1. How the Actual run compared to our Expected plan

Ticket 101: Bulldog tee for Tauhid Zaman (plan matched; the Boss repeated itself) · 80 Acceptable

  • What matched: The Boss called Inventory first, as planned, because the first question for a customer order is whether the shelf can fill it. Then came exactly the three delegations we wrote: Boss → Inventory → Accounting → Customer Service. Facilities was never called.
    • Inventory found 0 size S tees and that Bulldog Print Co was blocked by invoice 501 ($840, 3 days overdue as of 2026-08-31).
    • Accounting queued payment request #1, and nobody tried create_purchase_order. We predicted the purchase order tool would refuse anyway.
    • The Boss's status was waiting_approval, as expected. The Cash tab shows the predicted move: $3,400.00 → $2,560.00 when I approved #1.
  • What differed: it used 24 tool calls against our budget of 20, and 21 model requests against 18.
    • The Boss made 10 of those 24 calls. It read the ticket twice, and called check_cash and list_pending_approvals twice each (3 identical repeats, −9).
    • It also re-ran Inventory's check_stock and check_vendor_status. Our Boss prompt says "verify the specialists", and that instruction is what cost the points.
    • The same plan scored 96 in the Problem 5 test run, so the same prompts vary from run to run.
  • What our plan missed: "resolved" isn't the same as "Tauhid has a tee".
    • Paying invoice 501 resolved the ticket under our rule, but no agent came back to order the $8 tee once the vendor was unblocked.
    • Draft 1 honestly tells Tauhid there's no restock date.

Ticket 102: Rent for Elm City Properties (right agents, one extra handoff) · 62 Acceptable

  • What matched: The Boss called Facilities first, as planned. Facilities owns lease 1 and confirmed $2,400 due 2026-09-02 from the lease record, not the email.
    • Facilities then handed off to Accounting. That was the one specialist-to-specialist hop we predicted.
    • Inventory and Customer Service were correctly left out.
    • Accounting queued request #2 for $2,400, with the amount pulled from the database, and Facilities saved landlord draft 2.
    • The Cash tab shows $2,560.00 → $160.00 on approval, and lease 1's next due date moved to 2026-10-02.
  • What differed: after Facilities reported back, the Boss sent Accounting a second task ("review existing request 2… assess affordability"). That made 3 delegations instead of 2.
    • Accounting made 11 tool calls, fetching the shop date, ticket, lease and cash twice each.
    • It also called check_vendor_status, which our plan didn't expect.
    • The result was 22 calls against a budget of 16, and our lowest score.
  • What our plan got wrong: that "wasted" second pass produced the only cross-ticket advice of the run: pay overdue request #1 first, then the rent by 2026-09-02. That's the order I approved in, as the Cash tab shows.
    • Our plan scored each ticket alone. In reality, all three share one checking account, so a cross-ticket cash check deserved budget in the plan.

Ticket 103: Bulk hoodies for the Yale AI Club (most efficient; more cautious than planned) · 85 Efficient

  • What matched: The Boss called Inventory first. A discount is moot if the order can't be filled, and Inventory found 8 of 20 in size M (short 12) with the same blocked vendor.
    • Then came Accounting and Customer Service. Facilities wasn't called.
    • Nobody called request_payment or create_purchase_order, and the Boss's status was waiting_vendor, exactly as planned.
    • The Cash tab shows $0.00 for this ticket. Even after both approvals, the 12-hoodie restock would cost 12 × $22 = $264, more than the $160 left.
  • What differed: our plan expected the Boss to pick a bulk price from Accounting's margins and have Customer Service quote it.
    • Accounting did price it: check_pricing ×5, for 20 hoodies at list and at 5%, 10% and 15% off, plus the cost of restocking 12.
    • The Boss even checked 10% off itself, but then declined to quote any price while the full 20 couldn't be confirmed. So draft 3 says we "can't yet confirm… pricing".
    • The Boss also made one unexpected call (check_availability, −5), and the run used 27 tool calls against 22.
  • What it taught us: the agents followed "don't invent a discount policy" so strictly that the customer got no number.
    • Our resolve rule then marked the ticket resolved, because nothing was waiting on a human, even though the club has neither hoodies nor a price.

2. What would have been simpler as one agent with tools, and why

  • Ticket 102 is the clearest case. Rent touches one lease row and one action.
    • One agent could have done it with check_rent_due, check_cash, list_pending_approvals, request_payment and save_draft: five tool calls in one loop.
    • The team used 22 MCP calls, 18 model requests and 3 delegations. Each handoff meant re-typing facts into the task text, and then the receiver re-fetched them anyway: Accounting called check_rent_due twice for the same lease.
  • Ticket 101 nearly as much. The Boss re-ran Inventory's check_stock and check_vendor_status itself, so the Inventory delegation mostly duplicated work. A single "shop clerk" agent would have read stock, the vendor and cash once, queued #1, and drafted to Tauhid.
  • The evidence across all three runs: get_shop_today was called 12 times (4 per ticket) for a value that never changed (2026-08-31), and get_ticket 13 times. One agent reads each once.
  • Why: our three tickets are short, mostly linear lookups across a few tables. Each delegation costs a model loop plus a hand-written summary. The efficiency meter flagged exactly these costs: repeats, extra lookups, and date-only turns.
  • What the team still bought us:
    • Tool scoping as a second safety layer. Customer Service never had check_cash or check_pricing, so drafts 1 and 3 couldn't leak the $840 invoice or the $22 unit cost even by accident. But save_draft's guard blocks those anyway, so one agent with guarded tools keeps most of the safety.
    • Two voices that conflict: a warm customer voice and a skeptical controller.
    • Parallel work at higher ticket volume. For three tickets, one agent would have been simpler and cheaper.

The team would correctly queue a request, and approve_payment would refuse it every time. Nothing can mark a purchase order received, put the tee on the shelf, or deliver a message. All three drafts (to Tauhid, to Elm City Properties and to the Yale AI Club) sit unsent by design. receive_purchase_order (PO → received, inventory.qty += qty), advance_shop_date (human or system only), and a human-approved send_message with a delivery log. A Receiving role (or an Inventory extension) that wakes on expected_arrival and re-opens tickets like 101 to finish them.
Answering a discount request with an actual price. Ticket 103 ended with no quote. The records have no discount policy, and our prompts forbid inventing one. Accounting can compute 5%, 10% and 15% margins but no one is authorized to choose. So the Yale AI Club got "can't confirm pricing" and the ticket still resolved. A discount_policy table with a check_discount_policy tool (maximum % by quantity, margin floor). A human price-approval path like payments (request_price_approval / approve_price) shown as a card on the dashboard. A Sales / Merchandising agent that proposes a quote within policy for Customer Service to send.

What we'd change before the next run

  • Put the shop date and the facts already gathered into every delegation task. That would remove the date-only turns and the re-fetching tips flagged on all three tickets.
  • Tell the Boss to trust a specialist's sourced facts and spot-check only money figures. That's where 101's −9 for repeats came from.
  • Give the Boss one cross-ticket cash review instead of a second Accounting delegation (102), and budget for it in the plan.
  • Separate "desk work done" from "customer served", so tickets like 101 and 103 can be reopened when a restock arrives or a price policy exists.
